01 · The role

A small company is not a simple company

López S.A. · Pension-contribution collections for the unemployment-insurance administrator and the main pension funds · Santiago · Sep 2018 to Sep 2025

A firm of ~30 people collecting, under legal mandate, the pension contributions that employers left unpaid. A small niche with enormous density: every piece of data had an owner, a format, a deadline and a consequence.

Mine was a path, not a job title. I started on the administrative side of the operation, and the curiosity to understand every corner of the ecosystem led me into management control: every corner understood became a proposal, every proposal a systematised process, and every systematised process something someone else could run. That is how I reached the role I ended with.

In a large company this complexity is spread across departments. Here it converged on one point, and that point was me. Not because I did everything, but because I was the one who understood how everything connected: what effect a court ruling had on the client settlement, or a client's format change on the internal system.

That is why my working pattern became this, and it is the most transferable thing I have:

The density of the system

  • 4 institutional clients, each with its own format, channel, protocol and platform
  • 200+ types of procedural event in the life cycle of each lawsuit
  • Tens of thousands of active cases in the portfolio at the same time
  • 3 kinds of deadline at once: those committed to each client, the procedural ones and the financial ones, with daily interest if money stalls
  • A regulator (the Pensions Superintendency) behind every client report

Analyse · Decode · Systematise · Control

My value was in turning processes that depended on one person's knowledge into systems that work without them. I kept the part that cannot be systematised: the analysis, the design and the exceptions.

STEP 1

Analyse

Start from real data, not assumptions: explore the databases, cross sources and measure, to understand what is really happening before proposing anything.

STEP 2

Decode

Reverse-engineer the process: study the business rules, each client's requirements and the required formats until the exact logic is extracted, with its conditions, joins and edge cases.

STEP 3

Systematise

Turn that logic into tools: ready-made queries, templates, step-by-step manuals. The process becomes executable by anyone, with no prior knowledge and no risk of touching what is delicate.

STEP 4

Control

Progress indicators, quality checks and differentiated handling of exceptions. Deviations are caught early and with data, before they escalate.

I connected: general management · the clients' technical counterparts · systems development · paralegals and lawyers · the administrative team · accounting and settlements · the regulator, through the reports.

That cycle did include coordinating and handing over work. But the core was always the same: logic, data and design. And that mechanics travels to any context: if the process has rules, deadlines, data and exceptions, I know how to analyse it, decode it, systematise it and control it.

04 · Results

Four examples of what I contributed and what it produced

There were many more. These four are the ones that fit best on a card.

AUTOMATION

From 700 a month to 800 per person

Uncollectibility analysis was done by five people who shared it with court processing: a ceiling of 700 cases a month, and their core function neglected. I redesigned the process and the team structure, specified the logic for a tool that assembled case files in batches — my specs often travelled straight into SQL — and documented every step.

Four people fully dedicated, 800 cases a month per person, and the new positions paid for themselves with what those same case files recovered. The same engine generated up to 3,000 court filings in 10 minutes.

OPTIMIZATION

A lighter portfolio for everyone

The portfolio reached close to 90,000 active cases. Lawsuits against the same debtor were processed separately, duplicating costs and client fees. I designed the eligibility query and ran the case-consolidation process every month; and at the management table I defined a process-server assignment rule — one case per debtor, extending the result to the related ones — so we never paid twice for the same answer.

An average of 250 consolidation requests a month over a year, and a control of court deposits that used to live in personal spreadsheets: the overdue backlog dropped from 104 to 20. Everyone won: the operation spent less, debtors paid lower costs, and the team freed time for the cases that could actually be collected.

JUDGMENT

A calculation that "didn't make sense to me"

The contracts set fees by debt bracket, with decreasing percentages. The system's output didn't add up to me. I went to the contracts: the brackets were cumulative — like a progressive tax table — and the system wasn't applying them that way. I designed test cases with real collections, checked every scenario against my own calculation and coordinated the fix with development, iterating until they all matched.

The company had been under-billing its fees for years. Nobody asked me to look: the number didn't make sense, so I went to find out why.

CLOSURE

Closing well is also an achievement

In 2025, a legal change led the company to close. I was responsible for the technical closure of all four contracts: reconciling the portfolio, documenting each delivery and handing it over in order to the clients. They kept me on for two additional months as an external consultant to answer their questions about what was delivered.

Four portfolios delivered and reconciled: over 100,000 records across the contracts. With the main client, over 60,000 records at 99.3% reconciliation.
